## 4. What works today (search summaries; pages could not be opened from this session)

Web search worked, but this session could not open pages, so I rely on search summaries, many from trade or vendor sites. I found no brand whose polished generated cartoon people follow for fun. What people follow is a character with a clear personality inside a cheap, repeatable format: a vlog the character films, a mockumentary talking head, a group-chat serial, a puppet duo. One premise per video, the hook in the first seconds, the brand in the bio or as a cast member. The latest film does the opposite: glossy 3D look, twelve clips, six labels that explain the plot, two product cards. Label generated video everywhere and use only licensed-library or original music.

- People do not punish generated video as such; they punish video that looks low-effort: in Animoto's 2026 survey (about 450 US consumers and marketers, vendor data) the cues people named were robotic gestures (67%), unnatural voices (55%) and missing emotion (51%), and 36% said an AI video lowers their view of the brand.
- The generated series people chose to follow put a character with a personality inside a format that excuses the medium: selfie vlogs 'filmed by' a Bigfoot or a Yeti (since May 2025; about a minute of stitched 8-second clips of everyday chores) and the mockumentary talking heads of Neural Viz, whose creator writes, storyboards and acts every face himself.
- Glossy 3D cartoon is the most slop-crowded look on TikTok: Kapwing (data from May 2026) counted 59% AI slop in the first 500 videos shown to a new account and 97 of 100 videos under #cartoonkids, and my reading is that the current art-toy look sits in that neighbourhood.
- Brands are judged harder than creators: McDonald's Netherlands pulled a fully generated 45-second Christmas ad three days after launch in December 2025, Coca-Cola's 2024 ad drew the same anger, and an Ad Age discussion of 'Fruit Love Island' (300 million views in nine days, then called slop, mass-reported and stopped) said people accept AI for pure entertainment more than for brand use (I saw this only second-hand).
- Counter-evidence: in System1's ad tests viewers did not notice AI unless asked and 18 AI-assisted ads averaged 3.4 stars against 2.3 for all ads (a small, biased sample), while its 2026 Super Bowl review said the weak ads lacked a story and a character to root for.
- The brand characters people follow for fun (Duolingo's owl, Ryanair's talking plane, Scrub Daddy, Nutter Butter) post quick lo-fi bits built on current trends, the character is a personality and the product is rarely pitched; Crocs launched a mascot in 2026 that does not speak at all.
- The 2025-2026 shift is from single posts to small shows: 'Roomies' from the fintech Bilt (an ultra-short sitcom with the brand named only in the bio, two Webby People's Voice awards in 2026), 'The Group Chat' (one creator plays every part, roughly 100 million views over five episodes) and 'The Chit Show' (a million followers in a month off one catchphrase).
- Comedy that builds a following is one premise per video plus a repeatable bit, meaning a fixed setup, a recurring character and a line the audience waits for, and platforms now support it with series tools (TikTok mini-dramas for brands since June 2026, Meta testing 'Series').
- Hook: TikTok's own ad guidance says to put the hook inside the first six seconds and reports that 90% of ad-recall impact falls there, another TikTok guide says three seconds, and third-party claims of one to two seconds are unverified.
- Length: there is no agreed best length; Socialinsider's 2026 benchmark has videos under 30 seconds winning on engagement while longer ones collect more views, sketch creators run from about 20 to 90 seconds, and for an account with no followers I judge 10 to 30 seconds the safer start.
- Captions: estimates of sound-off viewing run from 60% to 90% and are poorly sourced, so spoken words should be burned in, but a label that explains the plot (the latest film has six) shows the joke does not carry itself (my judgement).
- Handmade is the late-2026 counter-trend: Levi's made a stop-motion film that took about 2,000 hours, Barbour and Thatchers went to Aardman, a Canva report says 78% prefer ads made by people, Gartner (1,006 US consumers) found 65% think brands publish too much AI content, and TikTok's 2026 trend report favours real experience over 'curated perfection'.

## 5. Craft and cost

The last film cost 47 dollars through habit: about 190 paid seconds of clips for a 45-second film. The same service sells an 8-second Veo 3.1 lite clip for 0.30 and a still for 0.022. A 15-25 second sketch can cost 0.7 to 3.2 dollars if work goes sound first, stills second, motion last, one take per shot. The glossy 3D toy look sits near the "shouts AI" end; cut-out moved in the edit, felt or clay puppets on twos, and a phone vlog hide the tells best. Voices: real people or a dialogue model, no lip-sync on rigid faces. A test of at most 1.6 dollars proves the look first. Web evidence is thin, mostly vendor blogs.

- Under a quarter of the paid footage reached the screen: 45 dollars at 0.235 a second is about 190 seconds of clips and the edit uses about 42 of them, because every shot was filmed twice at 1080p on the dearest model.
- The work order that saves most: write the sketch, record or generate the voices, cut an animatic from stills with the final sound for under half a dollar, and buy a moving clip only for a shot the animatic shows needs motion.
- The present glossy 3D toy look sits near the "shouts AI" end: smooth plastic skin, soft perfect light and slow smooth motion are the default output of image models, and clean surfaces hide no drift and no lip error.
- Best at hiding the tells, in order: cut-out or paper collage moved in the edit (no generated motion at all), felt, clay or puppet stop-motion stepped to 12 frames a second, then a phone vlog held by the character; a flat 2D cartoon is safe only when the edit moves it, since video models make its lines wobble.
- The founder's own words point the same way: on 15 Nov they wrote that the drawn style works better, and on 12 Nov that Veo is better (the word before "veo" in that message is unclear).
- Price facts from my free checks today: Veo 3.1 lite image-to-video costs 0.30 at 720p whether the clip is 4, 6 or 8 seconds, about six times cheaper per second than the Kling 4.0 1080p setting, and Veo 3.1 fast costs 0.60.
- Voices, option one, costs nothing and is the only truly real one: two people record the lines on a phone; a May 2026 Gallup-Bentley survey of 3,270 US adults found that 73% of those aged 18-29 call AI-made people or voices in ads unacceptable.
- Voices, option two: a dialogue speech model (0.0044 dollars for two lines) gives five readings of each line for cents so the funniest can be chosen, but adding lip-sync costs 0.04 to 0.50 a shot and is risky, because one sync vendor's own help pages say cartoon faces are generally not supported.
- Voices, option three: Veo's own speech is rated more natural than Kling's in the comparisons I found and its lips match by construction at no extra cost, but a report says the voice changes from clip to clip, so each character should speak in one clip only; a narrator or voice-over over flap mouths, hidden mouths or backs avoids lips altogether for cents.
- Recipe A, cut-out, about 0.7 dollars: 5 to 7 set-ups from about 18 stills, no video model, pose swaps, snaps and two or three mouth shapes moved in the edit, voices from people or the dialogue model; a still is redrawn only for a named defect, at most two rounds.
- Recipe B, felt puppets on twos, about 1.5 dollars: 5 shots of which 3 are Veo 3.1 lite 8-second clips at 720p from start stills, re-timed to 12 frames a second and upscaled in the edit, voice-over with no lip-sync, one take per shot and one retake budgeted for the whole video.
- Recipe C, character vlog with native voice, about 3.2 dollars (1.7 with lite): 3 Veo 3.1 fast 8-second clips at 720p, one speaker per clip talking to a hand-held phone, one take per shot and two retakes budgeted.
- Against the expensive way all three lose 1080p sharpness, the choice between two takes, and reference-locked faces and voices, so actions must stay small and frontal; A also loses fluid motion and true lip-sync, and Veo lite's quality in these styles is untested.
- Retake rule for every recipe: only for a defect on a written list (wrong character or prop, morphing, a cut inside the clip, wrong words) that the edit cannot hide, only after the start still or the prompt has been changed, once per shot, and a second failure turns the shot into a still with voice-over.
- First test, at most 1.6 dollars: one 8-second joke as three style stills, five voice readings plus a phone recording, the cut-out version, one Veo lite clip, one Veo fast clip with a spoken line and one lip-sync probe, judged against pass rules written beforehand; a person must judge the voice sample because the assistant cannot hear, and apart from that nothing goes to the founder until a video is finished, as they asked.
- Process rules: a written cap of 6 dollars per video checked before every paid call, one take by default (the "--takes 2" habit is in the skill file and the season notes, not in the code), every fixable thing fixed on 0.022 stills first, a free price check before each batch, and a "paid seconds on screen" figure reported with every delivery (last film about 22%, target above 70%).
